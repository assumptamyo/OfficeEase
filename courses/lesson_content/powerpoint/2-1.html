Lesson 2.1

An audience cannot read a slide and listen to a speaker at the same time. Text on a slide must therefore be brief, large and instantly legible from the back of the room. This lesson sets out the typographic rules that achieve this and the tools that apply them.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • apply the principles of contrast, hierarchy and brevity to slide text;
  • choose suitable fonts and sizes for titles and body text;
  • manage text boxes, list levels and AutoFit behaviour.

Three principles of slide text

Contrast
Text must stand out clearly from its background: dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark one. Mid-grey on white, or text laid over a busy photograph, cannot be read at a distance.
Hierarchy
Size and weight should tell the audience what to read first. The title is largest, the main points smaller, supporting detail smaller still.
Brevity
A slide carries key words, not sentences. The speaker supplies the explanation.

Sizes and the 6 × 6 rule

ElementRecommended sizeReason
Slide title32 to 40 ptRead first; states the point of the slide
Body text18 to 24 ptLegible from the back of a meeting room
Captions and sources12 to 14 ptPresent, but deliberately unobtrusive

The 6 × 6 rule is a practical limit: no more than about six bullet points on a slide and about six words in each. If a slide needs more, it is two slides.

Common mistakeReducing the font size until everything fits. When text will not fit at 18 pt, the answer is to remove words or split the slide, never to shrink the type.

Choosing fonts

  • Use no more than two fonts: one for headings and one for body text.
  • Prefer sans-serif fonts such as Calibri, Arial or Segoe UI, which stay clear on screens and projectors.
  • Use bold for emphasis. Avoid underline, which suggests a hyperlink, and long passages in capitals, which are slow to read.
  • Set the fonts once through Design > Variants > Fonts so that every slide uses the same pair.
TipAn unusual font may be missing from the computer you present on, and PowerPoint will substitute another. Either keep to standard fonts or embed them: File > Options > Save > Embed fonts in the file.

Text boxes and list levels

Insert a free text box with Insert > Text Box for labels and captions; use the layout's placeholders for the main content.

ActionHow
Demote a bullet to a sub-pointTab at the start of the line
Promote it againShift + Tab
New line within the same bulletShift + Enter
Align textCtrl + L, E or R
Line spacingHome > Line Spacing; 1.0 to 1.2 suits most slides

When a placeholder overflows, PowerPoint's AutoFit quietly shrinks the text. Click the AutoFit Options button that appears at the corner of the placeholder and choose Stop Fitting Text to This Placeholder, then shorten the text yourself.

Worked example: rescuing a crowded slide

  1. Start with a slide containing a full paragraph of 60 words in 14 pt.
  2. Identify the three ideas in the paragraph and rewrite each as a phrase of five or six words.
  3. Move the original paragraph into the Notes pane to use as your script.
  4. Set the title to 36 pt and the three points to 24 pt.
  5. Check the contrast by stepping back from the screen: every word should still be readable.

Key takeaways

  • Slide text must have strong contrast, a clear hierarchy and very few words.
  • Titles 32 to 40 pt, body text 18 to 24 pt.
  • The 6 × 6 rule: about six points of about six words.
  • Two fonts at most; cut text rather than shrink it.

Check your understanding

What does the 6 × 6 rule recommend?

About six bullet points per slide and about six words per point.

Your text overflows the placeholder at 20 pt. What should you do?

Reduce the number of words or split the content across two slides, rather than making the font smaller.

Which key turns a bullet point into a sub-point?

Tab, pressed at the start of the line. Shift + Tab promotes it again.
